## The Media Hype and the Distortion of Public Perception

Every year when the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) releases its latest liveability rankings, a familiar cycle unfolds across global media. Headlines proclaim Copenhagen or Vienna as urban paradises, while simultaneously slapping harsh "unliveable" labels on cities across tropical, arid, or developing nations. 

In recent days, social timelines and news feeds have been inundated with articles interpreting these rankings as definitive proof of civic failure. Local populations read these headlines and absorb a troubling narrative: *that their cities are inherently dysfunctional, misgoverned, or inferior.*

This media narrative sways public perception in dangerous ways. It creates unwarranted public cynicism, fosters national inferiority complexes, and distracts from real local urban victories. But before citizens internalize these low scores, a fundamental question must be asked:

**Is the EIU Index a genuine measure of urban health, or simply a private company's expatriate allowance calculator?**

> "There are three kinds of lies: lies, damned lies, and global liveability indexes."

---

## The Hook: Copenhagen’s Perfect Score vs. The Doorstep Test

The EIU 2026 rankings arrived with predictable fanfare. Topping the global leaderboard once again is Copenhagen, boasting perfect 100/100 scores across stability, education, and infrastructure. On paper, the Danish capital represents the zenith of human settlement—a serene urban oasis where public systems hum with frictionless precision.

Yet contrast this expert-approved perfection with a far more human counter-narrative. Author Chibundu Onuzo once framed what could be called the "doorstep test." Reflecting on her daily lived experience, she noted the hilarious yet revealing reality of frequently stepping around animal feces on doorsteps in highly rated London, compared to zero such occurrences in her home city of Lagos—a metropolis routinely relegated to the bottom tier of the EIU index.

This contrast cuts straight to the core issue: **Who actually decides what makes a city liveable?**

When global indexes reduce the chaotic, rich tapestry of human cohabitation into a static spreadsheet of 30 variables, whose reality are they actually measuring?

---

## Liveability for Whom? Scraping the Expatriate Façade

To understand why global city rankings feel so disconnected from the ground reality of median-income citizens, one must first demystify what the index was engineered for. The EIU Global Liveability Index is not a public service project, nor is it designed to advise municipal mayors on civic welfare.

It is a commercial product engineered for multinational corporations to calculate HR relocation packages, salary premiums, and **"hardship allowances"** for corporate executives moving abroad.

This foundational "knowledge-constitutive interest" shapes every metric in the evaluation. The index does not measure how well a city serves the average family born within its limits; it measures how comfortably a wealthy, relocating Western expatriate can replicate their baseline lifestyle in an unfamiliar environment.

Because the metric is tailored for corporate mobility, a city can achieve a near-flawless score even as its local residents face crippling housing unaffordability, widening wealth inequality, and severe public transit deficits. The math simply ignores the friction experienced by anyone outside the expatriate enclave.

---

## Section 1: The "Outsider's" Thermometer: The Climate Bias

The mathematical absurdities of the EIU methodology become immediately apparent when examining how it categorizes physical environment.

Under the "Culture & Environment" category—which commands a massive **25% weighting** of the entire overall index score—the EIU factors in metrics such as "humidity/temperature" and the explicit "discomfort of climate to travellers." By contrast, the entire **Education** category is assigned a meager **10% weighting**.

* **Culture & Environment**: 25% Total Weight (includes climate discomfort to foreign travellers)
* **Stability**: 25% Total Weight (26 out of 30 indicators graded by analyst subjectivity)
* **Healthcare**: 20% Total Weight
* **Infrastructure**: 20% Total Weight
* **Education**: 10% Total Weight

Rating climate in this manner systematically penalizes vibrant, modern metropolises located in tropical, arid, or cold regions—such as Kuala Lumpur, Singapore, or Brasilia—regardless of how well-governed, clean, or technologically advanced they are.

Grading a city down for immutable geographical traits is intellectually dishonest. The index evaluates weather strictly through the lens of an outsider's transient discomfort, ignoring that seasonal heat or humidity are completely normal background realities for local populations who have adapted their architecture, clothing, and daily schedules accordingly. Furthermore, it turns a blind eye to the reality that even top-tier temperate cities like Sydney or Vienna now routinely endure punishing, unmitigated summer heatwaves.

---

## Section 2: What "Stability" Overlooks: Expats vs. Locals

The "Stability" category accounts for another **25%** of the index and is evaluated across five primary indicators: prevalence of petty crime, violent crime, threat of terror, military conflict, and civil unrest.

While safety is undeniably fundamental to liveability, the EIU’s measurement of stability suffers from two profound flaws:

1. **Analyst Subjectivity**: Rather than relying on transparent, granular, and publicly audited statistical data, the index relies heavily on the subjective judgment of in-house EIU analysts and regional correspondents. In fact, **26 out of the 30 total indicators** across the index are assigned based on analyst discretion.
2. **Corporate Threat Orientation**: Because the index is calibrated for corporate risk assessment, its stability scores prioritize disruptions to commercial operations and multinational assets over the nuanced safety threats felt by local communities.

This expat-centric safety lens leaves massive blind spots. It ignores severe societal anxieties—such as the ubiquitous threat of school shootings and gun violence in American cities, rampant public loneliness epidemics, or gender-specific safety threats at night in major European capitals. A city where corporate headquarters run smoothly can score high on "stability," even if local women feel unsafe walking home from a subway station at night.

---

## Section 3: "Club Goods" over Public Welfare

The most glaring structural distortion in the EIU ranking is its pronounced **cosmopolitan bias** toward private infrastructure over public welfare.

When assessing Healthcare and Education, the index explicitly tracks the "availability of private healthcare" and the "availability of private education" alongside public options.

By elevating "club goods"—exclusive, premium services accessible primarily to corporate elites and high-net-worth individuals, such as international private schools and concierge medical clinics—the index measures a version of urban life reserved for the top tier of earners.

It actively downplays or ignores decaying public service infrastructure:

* Crumbling public school systems where local children receive inadequate instruction.
* Overburdened public healthcare networks with multi-month waiting lists.
* Hyper-inflated housing markets that price out teachers, nurses, and service workers.

When private international clinics allow an expatriate to bypass local healthcare queues, the index awards a high score—completely obscuring the systemic decay endured by median-income residents every day.

## Section 4: Restoring a Local Definition of "Liveability"

The EIU Global Liveability Index acts as a static "Best Student" award. It bestows an inherent, self-perpetuating advantage upon wealthy, temperate Western cities that inherited centuries of accumulated capital and mature physical infrastructure. This static framework keeps the same subset of European and Australasian cities permanently locked at the top of the leaderboards.

True liveability, however, cannot be captured by a proprietary, corporate-minded template exported from London.

### The Development Cycle: Structural Baggage vs. Inherited Capital

It is vital to acknowledge a pragmatic reality: developing cities are far from flawless. They often contend with heavy traffic, ongoing urban construction, environmental dust, infrastructure strain, and administrative friction.

However, this friction is not proof of civic failure—it is the unavoidable "baggage" required to attain long-term betterment. Developing cities carry these structural burdens precisely because they are actively navigating the necessary, unpleasant phase of the development cycle: lifting millions out of poverty, building foundational public transit, and expanding national industrial capacity.

Wealthy Western metropolises sitting atop the EIU leaderboard are not friction-free because of superior governance alone; they simply completed that heavy industrialization phase generations ago, inheriting centuries of accumulated capital while often offshoring their manufacturing footprint. Penalizing a growing city for the noise, dust, and friction of its active progress misinterprets vital economic development as urban dysfunction.

### Celebrating the "Most Improved"

Instead of rewarding static historical wealth, urban evaluation should shift toward measuring **policy progress relative to a city's own baseline**. When we scrape away the expatriate façade, we can begin celebrating genuine urban innovation across the Global South that the EIU's index ignores:

* **Bogotá, Colombia**: Transforming urban mobility by prioritizing pedestrian highways (Ciclovía) and rapid bus transit over private automobile lanes.
* **Seoul, South Korea**: Dismantling an elevated downtown highway to restore the Cheonggyecheon river, turning concrete congestion into a sprawling central urban waterpark.
* **Dhaka, Bangladesh**: Decentralizing clean energy by systematically integrating micro-grid rooftop solar systems across dense residential neighborhoods.

---

## Conclusion: Don't Let Corporate Metrics Define Civic Pride

When media headlines weaponize annual liveability scores to declare entire cities or nations "unliveable," citizens must remember what the data actually represents. The EIU index was never designed to reflect how deeply a community cares for its elderly, how vibrant its street culture is, or how resilient its local neighborhoods remain.

A city is not an executive hardship allowance calculation; it is a living home built by and for its people.

To evaluate cities honestly, we must reject corporate expatriate templates and embrace a standard of liveability that is **locally perceived, democratically shaped, and defined by the actual residents who live, work, and build community there.**
